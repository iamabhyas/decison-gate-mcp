# State regulation of insurance

Related index: `decision-gate://rules/usa-insurance`.

## McCarran-Ferguson

The McCarran-Ferguson Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1011–1015, is the federal starting point.

- Congress left regulation and taxation of the business of insurance to the states.
- A federal statute does not override a state insurance law unless that federal statute specifically relates to the business of insurance.
- The Sherman Act still applies to an agreement to boycott, coerce, or intimidate.
- Other federal antitrust laws apply to the business of insurance only to the extent the state does not regulate that conduct.

A decision that assumes one national insurance code is wrong. Ask which state's law applies.

## Who enforces it

Each state and the District of Columbia has an insurance department led by a commissioner or director. That office is the regulator for insurers and producers doing business there. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners writes model laws and accredits departments. A model is not law in a state until that state enacts it.

## Authority to transact

- An insurer needs a certificate of authority for the lines it writes in that state. Writing insurance without it is unauthorized insurance.
- A producer (agent or broker) must hold the license that state requires for the line being sold. Selling into another state usually requires a nonresident license or another authority that state recognizes.
- Many states also require the insurer to appoint the producer before the producer sells that insurer's policies.

## Rates and forms

States do not share one rating system. Depending on the state and the line, a rate or form may need prior approval, may be filed and then used, or may be used with later review. Surplus-lines placements are a separate path for coverage the admitted market does not provide, and they have their own eligibility and tax rules.

## Market conduct

State unfair-trade-practice laws, often based on the NAIC model, commonly prohibit:

- misrepresenting policy terms, benefits, or dividends
- false or misleading advertising
- twisting, which is misrepresenting a policy to induce a replacement
- unfair discrimination between insureds of the same class and hazard

Rebating and inducement rules differ. Some states still ban sharing commission or giving anything of value that is not in the policy. Others allow defined value-added services. Apply the statute of the state where the policy is sold.

## Solvency and insolvency

States supervise capital, reserves, and insurance holding companies. Risk-based capital and holding-company standards come from NAIC models that states adopt in their own words.

If an admitted insurer fails, a state life-and-health guaranty association or property-and-casualty guaranty association may cover part of the claim. Caps, lines covered, and claims that are excluded vary by state. Guaranty associations are not federal deposit insurance, and they generally do not back unlicensed insurers or surplus-lines carriers.
